Rossmann sales forecasting

856 stores, 48 days and one decision: how much stock to ship

I built a pipeline that turns 1,017,209 store-days into a 48-day daily sales forecast. No future leakage, chronological validation and an untouched holdout. The 65/35 ensemble cut RMSPE from 0.2283 to 0.1381.

Why

Rossmann operates 1,115 stores of different sizes, formats and competitive environments. Daily demand follows a strong weekly cycle, holidays and promotions. A 48-day forecast supports inventory planning, staffing and promotion scheduling.

This is not a leaderboard project. The goal was a process that can be defended: data, SQL, features, validation, error analysis and a decision. The holdout is internal — the last 47 days of the Kaggle training data — because the competition test labels are private.

Data

After joining train.csv and store.csv: 1,017,209 rows, 1,115 stores, 942 days and 33 columns. The test set has 41,088 store-days for 856 stores. History runs from 1 January 2013 to 31 July 2015.

Eight automated quality checks passed: no duplicate store-date pairs, every store matched store.csv, no negative sales, a contiguous date range and a submission row count that matches the test set. The project has 17 pytest tests.

Closed days account for 172,817 store-days (17.0%). They stay in the data because they carry information about opening patterns, but they are excluded from RMSPE. Separately, 54 store-days have Open = 1 and zero sales, and 11 missing Open values in the test set were filled with a business rule.

Pipeline

Code lives in src/; notebooks are for narrative and results. The order is fixed: data preparation, SQL layer, features, validation and training, forecast, dashboard export.

  • src/data_preparation.py — validation, cleaning, store join, parquet and a quality report.
  • sql/create_tables.sql and analytical_queries.sql — 13 queries with JOIN, GROUP BY, CTEs and window functions.
  • src/features.py — 46 calendar, store, promotion and historical features.
  • src/train.py — three rolling-origin folds, six models and the ensemble; src/predict.py — refit and submission.
  • src/dashboard_export.py — eight CSV tables for the Power BI star schema.

The key methodological decision is direct multi-horizon forecasting. History is frozen at the origin, and the model receives a 48-day horizon with features computed only from what was known on the forecast date. It never sees future Sales or Customers, and lags are not updated recursively, because that compounds errors.

Validation uses three 48-day folds plus a holdout from 15 June to 31 July 2015. There is no random train_test_split. Every model is scored on the same dates and store-days.

Model

I compared six single models and one ensemble. Baselines are historical average and seasonal naive. The statistical layer is OLS with HC3 errors and SARIMAX(1,0,1)(1,1,1,7) for Store 1. The ML layer is Random Forest and XGBoost, trained globally across stores.

SARIMAX does not scale to 1,115 separate series, so it is a case study and a reference point, not a production model. XGBoost and Random Forest receive the same 46 features; they differ in how they handle non-linearity and interactions.

The final model is 0.65 × XGBoost + 0.35 × store-weekday historical average. The 0.65 weight was selected on a 0.50–0.90 grid using folds 1–3 only. The holdout was scored once, after the decision was frozen.

RMSPE = sqrt( mean( ((y − ŷ) / y)² ) ) ensemble = 0.65 × XGBoost + 0.35 × hist_avg(store, weekday) error scored only for Open = 1 and Sales > 0

RMSPE penalises relative errors, so small stores weigh as much as large ones. It is the competition metric, not a purchasing decision metric.

Result

The holdout has 45,852 scored store-days after removing closures and zeros. The ensemble reached RMSPE 0.1381, MAE PLN 827, WAPE 11.6% and bias −7.9%. This is an internal holdout, not Kaggle — it is not directly comparable to the leaderboard.

Against the historical-average baseline, RMSPE falls by 39.5%. Against XGBoost alone, it falls by 19.4%. The gain is not confined to one metric: MAE drops from PLN 1,008 to 827, WAPE from 14.1% to 11.6%, and bias from −10.8% to −7.9%.

Error

I broke the error down by store type, weekday, promotion and sales decile. The highest RMSPE is in store types a (0.1427) and c (0.1353), the lowest in b (0.0976) — but type b has only 799 holdout store-days. Every type is under-forecast, from −2.6% for b to −8.6% for d.

Within the week, Saturday is the weakest (0.1521) and Wednesday the most stable (0.1272). Sunday shows 0.1744, but only 193 store-days are scored because 97.5% of stores are closed. Promotion raises MAE (PLN 1,046 vs 620) but not RMSPE (0.1401 vs 0.1363), because promotion days have a higher sales level.

The error is U-shaped across the sales distribution: the lowest decile has RMSPE 0.1753, the highest 0.1696, and the middle 0.1129–0.1341. The model struggles most at the edges — small stores make every zloty count, while large stores carry a bigger absolute error.

Dashboard

Power BI Desktop was not available in the environment, so there is no .pbix file. Instead of fabricating one, I delivered a star schema, 30+ DAX measures, three mockups and eight CSV exports that can rebuild the dashboard in a few minutes.

Page one covers sales, stores and store types; page two covers promotions, holidays and competition; page three covers actual vs predicted, RMSPE and error analysis. Every KPI is computed from the same data as the model.

Limitations

  • No external data. Weather, footfall and local events are missing, so the model cannot explain demand shocks.
  • Short history. 2.5 years is thin for stable annual seasonality; the holdout falls in summer, when the sales level is high.
  • Bias −7.9%. The model systematically under-forecasts the level. Calibration could reduce it, but it was not part of fold selection and I do not want to fit it to the holdout.
  • Promotion is correlation. Higher sales during promotions do not prove causation; there is no control group or experiment.
  • No PBIX and no Kaggle upload. The dashboard is a specification, and the submission is ready but not uploaded — it needs an account and approval.

The next step is level calibration on validation and external variables, not another model. Without that, the ensemble remains an honest but not the best possible tool.
